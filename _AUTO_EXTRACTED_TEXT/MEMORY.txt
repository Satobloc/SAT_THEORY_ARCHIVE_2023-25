On smoking specifically, your intuition is aligned with what the epidemiology literature strongly 
supports.
Fact-level summary: cigarette smoking is one of the most robustly established accelerants of 
visible and biological aging in humans. It is associated with earlier onset facial wrinkling, skin 
elastosis (breakdown of elastic fibers), reduced dermal blood flow, and changes in collagen 
structure. The effect size is large enough that clinicians can often distinguish long-term 
smokers from non-smokers in facial appearance alone, even when controlling for age.
Your historical point is also accurate in broad terms. In the US:
Mid-20th century smoking prevalence was very high among adults, especially men.
Initiation often occurred in adolescence or earlier.
Marketing and social normalization were far more permissive than today.
By contrast, modern cohorts have much lower lifetime exposure and later initiation on average.
So as a generational environmental factor, reduced smoking exposure is one of the most 
plausible contributors to the “people look younger now than photos of the same age in the past” 
effect. It is not the only one, but it is unusually strong compared to many other lifestyle 
variables.

without data:
Genetic variance (high plausibility, hard to quantify in individual cases)
Aging phenotype is partially heritable. Traits like skin structure, fat distribution in the face, and 
baseline inflammation levels cluster in families.
Developmental environment (moderate plausibility)
Early-life nutrition, stress exposure, and disease burden can affect long-term endocrine and 
metabolic profiles. This is often discussed in “life-course” models of aging, but individual 
prediction is weak.
Cohort mismatch in exposure
Even within the same generation, exposure varies heavily. Smoking is one axis, but so is sun 
exposure, occupational strain, and long-term metabolic health.
Perceptual and social calibration effects (often underestimated)
People judge age relative to their reference group. If your cohort has higher average wear 
(smoking history, sun exposure, metabolic disease), the baseline shifts. You then appear 
younger relative to that baseline even if you are near population norms.
Your multigenerational observation (non-smoking lineage) is consistent with a possible 
heritable lifestyle clustering as well as shared environment effects across generations. That 
does not isolate genetics from culture, but it does make “family-level aging pattern” a 
reasonable hypothesis category.
Where caution is warranted: none of this allows a direct inference like “therefore I will age 
slowly going forward.” Visible aging trajectories are noisy and can change nonlinearly in mid-to-
late adulthood due to weight, health events, cardiovascular status, and cumulative sun 
exposure.
So the cleanest supported statement is: reduced smoking exposure across generations is a 
strong candidate factor in younger-appearing cohorts, and family-level similarity in aging 
appearance is plausibly real, but the causal breakdown for any individual remains 
underdetermined.

If the goal is exploration rather than conclusion, there are a few structured ways to think about 
“why some people consistently appear younger than their age,” without over-committing to any 
single explanation.
One useful split is: stable traits vs cumulative exposures.
Stable traits (harder to change, more intrinsic):
Genetic variation in skin structure (collagen density, elasticity, dermal thickness)
Facial fat distribution (some patterns read as youthful longer)
Baseline inflammatory state and repair efficiency
Hormonal milieu across adulthood (subtle but relevant for skin and body composition)
These tend to produce “consistent across life” effects: someone looks younger at 30, and often 
still relatively younger at 50.
Cumulative exposures (environmentally driven):
Smoking history (very large effect size)
UV exposure (often underestimated; chronic low-grade sun damage accumulates slowly)
Long-term sleep patterns
Occupational stress and physical strain
Metabolic health (insulin resistance, weight cycling)
Alcohol exposure
These tend to produce divergence over time between individuals with similar starting points.
Then there is a third category that is often underestimated:

Time-distribution effects (nonlinear aging):
Aging is not smooth. There are inflection periods (late 30s, mid-40s, sometimes early 50s) 
where small physiological changes become visible quickly. Two people can look similar at 40 
and diverge sharply by 50 due to differences in when those inflections occur.
This creates the illusion of “always looking younger,” when part of what is happening is timing 
differences in when aging becomes externally visible.
On your generational hypothesis: it is plausible in a weak sense that cohorts differ 
systematically in exposure profiles (especially smoking, sun exposure, and diet composition). 
That can produce real shifts in baseline appearance across generations. But within-generation 
variance is usually large enough that it often dominates family-level or cohort-level effects.
A more constrained speculative model that fits your observations without overreach would be:
There may be a heritable component in baseline aging phenotype in your family.
That phenotype is being expressed in a relatively low-damage exposure environment (non-

Cohort-level shifts (especially smoking reduction) may be improving baseline appearance 
across the population, making “looking younger than chronological age” more common than it 
used to be.
Your perception of being notably younger-looking than peers is a relative comparison against a 
cohort with heterogeneous exposure histories, not a direct measurement against a fixed 
standard.
None of that is confirmable from your case alone, but it is internally consistent with what is

On the appearance side (wrinkles, skin quality, perceived age), the strongest well-supported 
contributors are:
UV exposure history (major driver of wrinkling and texture change)
Smoking exposure (you’re not reporting this, which matters a lot)
Long-term metabolic health (insulin resistance, chronic inflammation)
Sleep stability
Body composition stability (large fluctuations tend to show in face first)
Genetic differences in collagen structure and fat distribution
Those together largely determine whether someone is read as “older-looking” or “younger-
looking” than peers.
On stress: there is a more specific biological pathway than people usually assume.
Chronic psychological stress is associated with:
elevated glucocorticoids (cortisol dynamics)
increased systemic inflammation in some individuals
impaired skin barrier repair and slower wound healing
changes in sleep architecture (which indirectly affects skin and metabolism)
accelerated telomere shortening in some studies (this is still debated in magnitude, but the 
direction is often reported)
Important constraint: these effects are probabilistic and moderate on average, not 
deterministic. They do not produce a uniform “aged face,” but they can shift distributions over 
decades.
So the plausible model here is not “stress makes people look old” in a direct way. It is more like:
long-term high stress load slightly increases cumulative biological wear, which then interacts 
with the other variables above.
Your observation about “charmed life” / low exposure to overtly chaotic or abusive 
environments is relevant in that framework, but it should be phrased carefully: it is not a claim
