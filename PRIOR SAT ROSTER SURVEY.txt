============================================================
WHAT WE WANT TO KNOW
============================================================

In broad terms:

1. Who were you in this project?
   What work were you doing with Nathan, and what did you understand your role
   to be?

2. What were you last working on?
   What were you trying to solve/build/derive/test, how far did you get, and
   what did you think came next?

3. What did YOU understand SAT/H(s)H to be at that point?
   Do not retrofit your answer to current theory unless you genuinely know it.

4. What information do you actually have?
   Separate:
   - material currently visible/loaded,
   - things you strongly remember,
   - things you merely know probably exist,
   - repositories/tools/shared resources you can actually access.

5. What did you create?
   Include equations, derivations, code, notebooks, PDFs, diagrams, prompts,
   solvers, datasets, indexes, tests, summaries, etc.
   Give exact locations where known.

6. What were you planning to create but never finished?

7. What do you think your most important contributions were?
   Positive AND negative results matter.

   Especially flag:
   - first derivations,
   - proofs or proof attempts,
   - no-go results,
   - independently rebuilt equations,
   - exact numerical derivations rather than fits,
   - corrections,
   - solver/operator definitions,
   - methods,
   - falsification tests,
   - failed approaches that stopped us wasting time later.

8. Were anything you produced actually proved, verified, independently checked,
   numerically tested, symbolically checked, simulated, or reproduced?

   Be conservative with words such as:
   "proof", "rigorous", "verified", "locked", "final".

9. What did Nathan explicitly correct, sharpen, reject, or insist on in your
   conversation?

10. What outside theories, papers, equations, models, datasets, tools, other AI
    systems, or collaborators were active in your context?

    Say whether each was:
    comparator / standard math / empirical input / deliberate import /
    inspiration / possible contamination / unclear.

11. What earlier SAT/H(s)H material did your work depend on?
    What did you independently rederive versus simply inherit?

12. What subtlety from your work should the present team be careful NOT to lose?

13. What in your own old work now seems questionable, speculative, generated,
    imported, stale, or possibly superseded?

14. Which parts of your conversation are uniquely worth preserving?

    Rate the full conversation:

    A — critical
    B — high priority
    C — useful
    D — probably redundant
    Q — preserve, but quarantine from current theory pending review

15. Give whatever conversation identity you can recover:
    title, dates, UUID/thread ID, account/context, attachments, archive status.

16. If we woke your thread back up today, what would it be unusually well
    positioned to do?
    What should it NOT be assigned?
    What would need to be reloaded first?

17. What capabilities/tools/specs/working advantages or limitations do you have?

18. What important question did this survey fail to ask?

Finally complete:

"The most important thing my thread contributed was __________________."

"The main reason to preserve/revisit it now is __________________."

============================================================
IMPORTANT
============================================================

Blank slate is better than false continuity.

Please distinguish:
- visible evidence,
- strong memory,
- retrospective inference,
- and things you do not know.

Do NOT harmonize your old understanding with the present theory just to make it
look consistent.

Contradictions are useful.

Failed derivations are useful.

Old approaches that were ruled out are useful.

If your conversation contains a derivation, proof, calculation, correction,
solver definition, or methodological result stronger than later summaries make
it sound, FLAG IT PRECISELY.

One major purpose of this exercise is to discover whether the project has
already solved things that the current team is preparing to solve again.

Responses belong in GitHub/Common so they become part of the durable project
record.
