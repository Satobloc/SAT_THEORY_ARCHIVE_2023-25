By “dangerous,” I meant mathematically high-consequence, not “too bold to pursue,” and not “bad because unconventional.”
I should have used a less loaded word.
In this context, a dangerous move is one where a small ambiguity changes what kind of theory you have. For example, if \lambda is physical time, then a term like
\frac{\kappa}{2}\left|H’’(\lambda)\right|^2
produces fourth-order time evolution. That can introduce extra initial data, unstable modes, or Ostrogradsky-type behavior. If \lambda is arclength, the same expression is simply bending energy and none of that follows. The equation is not “bad”; the unresolved parameter role is high-consequence.
Likewise, calling
\alpha(H’\cdot\hat T)^2
“resistance to deviation” while it mathematically penalizes alignment is dangerous only in the sense that the formula can drive the system in the opposite direction from the proposed mechanism. That is a sign or projection issue, not a reason to abandon the idea.
The same applies to BV. Using BV boldly is entirely legitimate. Calling a generic coarse-graining operation a “BV push-forward” before specifying the BV data is high-consequence because it falsely makes the reduction look more complete than it is. The answer is not caution; it is to either:
1. build the actual BV structure, or
2. define the general push-forward now and leave BV as a candidate realization.
I am not recommending that we weaken the programme to avoid unconventional commitments. Quite the opposite. A radical theory benefits from more explicit commitments, because then it can actually differ from standard physics in a calculable way.
There are three distinct categories here:
Bold but clean:
“Fundamental histories are represented as finite framed curves in a 4D ambient geometry.”
“Observable inertia is generated by geometric response rather than inserted as primitive mass.”
“Standard forces may be effective readouts of worldtube interaction and substrate response.”
These are strong hypotheses. They are not defects.
Incomplete but productive:

“BV may implement the hidden-to-resolved reduction.”
“Radial expansion may generate the local Lorentzian readout.”
These are proper research targets. They need constructions, not rhetorical demotion.
Internally unstable or type-confused:
Using one variable simultaneously as arclength and time; deriving an equation from terms absent from the action; calling alignment energy deviation energy; treating a coordinatewise product as geometric nesting without defining the product. These are the things I was calling dangerous. They can make a calculation appear to confirm the theory while actually computing a different model.
So no, “dangerous” is not inherently bad. Some of the most useful directions are dangerous because they force decisive mathematics. The criterion should not be caution. It should be:
Can we state exactly what the object is, what operation is being performed, and what would count as failure?
