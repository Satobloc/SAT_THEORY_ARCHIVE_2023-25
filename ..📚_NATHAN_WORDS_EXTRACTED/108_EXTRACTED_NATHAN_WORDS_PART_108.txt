A natural language to describe exchange and twisting of filaments is the mathematics of braid groups. If we imagine moving filament segments around each other (in space or in spacetime), their world-lines can form braided structures. The Artin braid group $B_n$ encodes the topology of exchanging $n$ strands pairwise. In the SAT context, each filament carries a twist label (τ-state), and when two filaments exchange or cross, the system’s state can pick up a phase or follow a rule depending on those labels. This is directly analogous to the theory of anyons in 2D topological physics: anyons are particles with exotic exchange phases, described by representations of the braid group rather than just the permutation group. In fact, $\mathbb{Z}_3$ twist charges can be seen as Abelian anyons – exchanging two identical $\mathbb{Z}3$ charges can induce a rotation of the combined state by an angle (a phase $e^{2\pi i/3}$, for example). Formally, one can introduce braid operators $b_i$ that swap filament $i$ and $i+1$; these satisfy the braid relations (e.g. $b_i b{i+1} b_i = b_{i+1} b_i b_{i+1}$) and act on the twist-state of the system. If the twist labels were truly anyonic, braiding two τ’s might multiply their state by a root-of-unity phase. In a ribbon category, objects (here, filament state labels like τ₀, τ₁, τ₂) can be depicted as ribbons; one has well-defined operations for braiding (over-crossing or under-crossing two ribbons) and for adding a twist (a $360^\circ$ rotation of a ribbon, which in the algebra corresponds to a special morphism $\theta$ on that object). The most general framework encompassing these ideas is that of braided fusion categories, which is a category-theoretic formalism for anyons and topological charges. In a braided fusion category, one has objects (labels like the twist states) that can fuse according to specified rules, and one has braiding operators that exchange objects, all satisfying consistency (hexagon and pentagon) axioms. For example, a pointed fusion category based on $\mathbb{Z}3$ (with possibly a nontrivial 3-cocycle twist) would exactly have the properties we need: three simple objects (other than the identity) of order 3 under fusion, and abelian braiding statistics. If one further equips it with a ribbon (twist) structure, it becomes a modular tensor category suitable for a 2+1D topological field theory. The twist in category theory is an operator $\theta_X$ that represents a $360^\circ$ rotation of the object $X$; in a physical anyon model, $\theta_X = e^{2\pi i s_X}$ where $s_X$ is the spin/statistical angle of that anyon. In our filament model, a full $360^\circ$ twist of a filament might be trivial ($\theta{\tau}=1$ if a full rotation restores the filament) or could yield a phase if the filament’s twist state has extrinsic meaning (in planar diagrams, a full twist sometimes equals a central phase). Notably, modular categories provide a dictionary to topological quantum field theories (TQFTs): given the category, one can construct a TQFT (à la Turaev–Viro or Reshetikhin–Turaev) that reproduces the fusion and braiding in a quantum amplitude context. For instance, a TQFT based on the $\mathbb{Z}_3$ fusion category would have particle types corresponding to our τ states, with path integrals enforcing the rule that a triple of different τ’s can form a closed loop (handle) with nonzero amplitude, whereas other combinations are confined. Category theory also formalizes duals (particle–antiparticle) and evaluation (annihilation of a particle with its dual yields the vacuum), which is exactly the twist–antitwist cancellation rule. These ideas reassure us that there is a rigorous algebraic backbone available: one can treat SAT’s twist filaments as morphisms in a braided monoidal category, where composition (joining filaments) is only nonzero when the twist sum is neutral, and braiding (crossing) is a well-defined operation. Such a category can be seen as a skeleton for a field theory, encoding the symmetry and topological interactions.
