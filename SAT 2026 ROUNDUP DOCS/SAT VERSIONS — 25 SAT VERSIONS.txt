Because the five points are not really independent assumptions. They form a chain.

If you reject (1), then you're claiming direct access to reality-in-itself, which seems impossible to justify.

If you reject (2), then observation itself becomes inaccessible, and science cannot even begin.

If you reject (3), then observation cannot be represented, compared, accumulated, or communicated.

If you reject (4), then the map ceases to be a map. A representation that does not preserve relevant relations is merely a code.

If you reject (5), then you need some alternative language that preserves relations at least as faithfully as geometry.

The reason geometry keeps reappearing isn't that geometry is magical.

It's that relations are geometry, at least insofar as we can access them.

Distance.

Adjacency.

Continuity.

Containment.

Intersection.

Curvature.

Orientation.

Connectivity.

Those are all relational statements.

So the chain becomes surprisingly hard to escape.

What's interesting is that most scientists probably accept all five propositions implicitly.

They may not formulate them that way, but science as practiced generally assumes:

observations are available,
observations can be represented,
representations should preserve structure,
and coherent structure is preferable to incoherent structure.
The disagreement comes later, when deciding what counts as the most faithful representation.
For SAT, the answer is:

The highest-fidelity representation we possess is the full spacetime history.
Not because spacetime is ontology.
Not because Minkowski is sacred.

But because that's where the greatest amount of empirically constrained relational structure already resides.

Science doesn't actually assume the universe is coherent.

Science assumes that if there is coherence available to be found, then preserving observed relations is the best way to discover it.

That's a weaker assumption.

The universe could ultimately turn out to be fragmented, contextual, observer-dependent, or stranger than any of us imagine.

But if it possesses any stable structure at all, a faithful map should reveal more of it than a less faithful one.

Draw more of the map.
Not because geometry guarantees truth.
