For a Giant Impact Hypothesis–scale collision (a Mars-sized body striking the proto-Earth at ~10 km/s or more), “white hot” is not just plausible—it’s unavoidable over large fractions of the flow. The useful way to think about it is not a single temperature, but a very steep, rapidly evolving gradient tied to shock physics and decompression.

Right at contact, shock pressures reach hundreds of gigapascals. That drives temperatures in the immediate impact interface into the ~8,000–20,000 K range. In that regime, silicate rock is not just molten; it’s partially vaporized and significantly ionized. You’d get a luminous, plasma-like state—optically bright, with emission skewed toward white/blue-white because of the high temperature and ionization.

As the shock wave propagates outward and upward, material decompresses. That decompression causes rapid cooling, but “cooling” here still means thousands of kelvin. The bulk of the ejecta plume and the central rebound jet would likely sit in the ~3,000–6,000 K range. That’s classic incandescent-to-white-hot silicate melt: visually orange → yellow → white depending on local temperature and optical depth.

The “central rebound droplet” you’re aiming to depict is essentially a transient jet formed by pressure release and momentum focusing (similar in principle to a Worthington jet, but at planetary scale). Its temperature structure would be stratified:
	•	Core of the jet: hottest material, potentially still partially vaporized (≥6,000 K), bright white with some plasma effects—glow, slight translucency, and possibly a faint halo from ionized vapor.
	•	Outer layers of the jet: molten silicate (~2,500–4,000 K), appearing bright orange to yellow. This is where you’d get the fluid, splash-like morphology.
	•	Spray and droplets: rapidly cooling as they expand, dropping through red-orange into darker tones as they radiate heat and begin to quench.

Around the crater, you’d see a radial gradient:
	•	Inner basin: largely molten or even supercritical silicate fluid immediately post-impact—visually very bright, with minimal solid crust.
	•	Rim and beyond: fractured, partially melted crust. Temperatures fall below ~2,000 K, so you start to see darker, solidifying material with glowing fissures.

Two additional effects matter for realism:

First, radiative transfer. At these temperatures, the scene is self-illuminating. Shadows are soft and filled by the intense glow of surrounding նյութ. The brightest regions would bloom slightly (overexposed look), especially in a “high-speed photography” aesthetic.

Second, vapor and atmosphere. The impact generates a dense silicate vapor cloud mixed with any pre-existing atmosphere. This produces:
	•	Luminous, turbulent plumes
	•	Shock-heated gas that can briefly exceed the melt temperature locally
	•	Possible electrical activity (not lightning in the usual sense, but charge separation in an ionized plume)

So, to answer your intuition directly: yes—parts of it would be white hot, especially the core of the rebound jet and the immediate impact interface. The outer splash should look like fully molten liquid, not crusted, with color transitioning from white/yellow at the hottest points to orange/red as it expands and cools.

A Worthington jet is the narrow, upward spike of fluid that shoots up immediately after a surface is struck and then rebounds—classically seen when a drop hits water.

The sequence is straightforward in fluid-dynamics terms:
	•	An impact (a droplet, object, etc.) creates a cavity in the liquid.
	•	The surrounding fluid rushes inward to fill that cavity.
	•	That inward flow converges at the center and is redirected upward due to momentum focusing.
	•	The result is a thin, fast vertical jet, often ending in a small droplet at the tip.

The effect was first studied in detail by Arthur Mason Worthington in the late 19th century using early high-speed photography.

What matters for your impact visualization is the mechanism, not the scale. The same basic physics—cavity collapse and momentum focusing—applies across many orders of magnitude:
	•	Millimeter scale: a raindrop hitting a puddle.
	•	Meter scale: an object hitting water or mud.
	•	Planetary scale: a hypervelocity impact briefly behaving like a compressible fluid system.

In a giant impact, the “fluid” is not water but shock-softened rock and melt (and even vapor). The impact excavates a transient cavity, and as pressure releases, material collapses inward and is redirected upward, forming a central rebound jet. It’s not identical to a lab-scale Worthington jet—because compressibility, shock waves, and phase changes dominate—but it’s a useful analogy for why you get that upward “splash column” at all.

So when you describe a “central rebound droplet,” you’re essentially scaling up the Worthington-jet idea to a regime where the jet is molten silicate and partially vaporized rock rather than liquid water.

At giant-impact energies you don’t get a simple “air blown away → vacuum” outcome. You get a short-lived, violently restructured atmosphere: compressed, heated, partially ionized, and mixed with rock vapor.

Start with the shock. The impact launches a hemispheric shock through the atmosphere. Ahead of the ejecta curtain, air is strongly compressed and heated; temperatures can reach a few thousand kelvin in the lower atmosphere and higher in the immediate shock layer. Behind the front, pressure can drop rapidly as material expands upward, so there are transient low-density pockets, but not a stable vacuum. The atmosphere is being driven outward, not removed instantaneously.

Close to the impact cavity, conditions are extreme. You have a dense silicate vapor plume (rock vapor) plus whatever atmospheric gases were entrained. Near the jet core and the inner plume, temperatures overlap the melt/vapor regime you asked about earlier (several thousand to >10,000 K locally). That produces a luminous, partially ionized column—not a clean, transparent vacuum, but a glowing, optically thick mixture of vapor and melt droplets. The brightest regions would be around the central jet and the inner walls of the transient cavity.

Farther out, the physics starts to resemble large explosions in air, but scaled up:
	•	The rising plume forms a buoyant, turbulent column that entrains surrounding air.
	•	As it expands and cools, it develops a cap-like structure—analogous to a mushroom cloud, though here it’s laden with molten droplets and rock vapor, not just ash and hot gas.
	•	There will be strong shear and vortex rings around the plume margins, giving that rolled, cauliflower texture.

Condensation clouds are also expected. As the shock front moves outward, it can produce a brief pressure drop behind the front, causing moisture (if any ocean/atmosphere is present) to condense into a halo or ring cloud—similar in mechanism to the condensation clouds seen around large explosions, just vastly larger and mixed with silicate vapor.

Electrical activity is very likely. You have:
	•	Ionized gas (plasma regions),
	•	Intense turbulence and particle collisions,
	•	Charge separation in ash/melt droplets.

That combination supports lightning-like discharges within the plume and along its edges. It wouldn’t look exactly like a terrestrial thunderstorm, but you’d expect branching, high-energy flashes embedded in the glowing column and surrounding cloud.

So visually, for your scene:
	•	No clean vacuum around the impact site.
	•	A bright, plasma-tinged central column embedded in a dense, incandescent vapor plume.
	•	Surrounding turbulent cloud structures with rolling edges (mushroom-like, but more chaotic and luminous).
	•	Possible faint condensation halos at larger radii.
	•	Intermittent electrical discharges within the plume.

If anything, the atmosphere makes the scene more complex and luminous, not emptier.
