SAT GEOMETRIC SOLVERS

Yes. I think that should become a separate locked layer of the project, independent of whether SAT/H(s)H survives.

I’d freeze the taxonomy roughly like this:

**1. Universal Indicatrix (UI/TX)**
A dimension-general relative-frame / metrology solver.

Its irreducible construction is not the sphere. It is two coincident sets of axes with a controllable relative state. The essential operator is relative scaling; rotation is an additional native operator; unit spheres are optional calibration/visualization surfaces.

For (UI_n):

[
F_1,F_2\cong \mathbb R^n,
]

with relative transformation something like

[
G(\lambda)=D(\lambda)R(\lambda),
]

where (D) handles expansion/contraction and (R\in SO(n)) handles rotation. In the simplest isotropic version,

[
D=q(\lambda)I.
]

Its original purpose was geometric comparison. Its emergent strength is broader: it is a reference space in which extension and rotation are native variables/operators. It can work forward, backward, or variationally:

[
\text{controls}\to\text{geometry},
]

[
\text{geometry}\to\text{controls},
]

[
L+\text{partial controls}\to\text{forced geometry},
]

and it can project, reduce dimension, sweep surfaces, compare ruler systems, decompose trajectories, and so forth.

It is Hilbert-space-like in function, and appropriate spaces of UI histories can literally be given Hilbert-space structure.

The spheres are optional. The doubled frame is partly bookkeeping. The invariant content is the relative transformation.

**2. Proto-UI comparison machine**
Not a separate solver so much as the original intended use of UI.

Two curves are encoded on coupled surfaces; radial traversal plus controlled expansion/contraction creates coincidence events permitting a switch from one encoded curve to the other, producing a composite trace.

This matters historically because it tells us what UI was initially supposed to accomplish: force relationships between mathematical structures geometrically.

Its weakness was excessive freedom: timing, phase, ray choice, parametrization, switching point, etc. could determine the answer. It did not constrain the comparison tightly enough.

That failure led directly to—

**3. Whirligig / Donut**
A constrained geometric comparison/composition solver.

This is the machine specifically designed to do what proto-UI could not reliably do: encode two structures faithfully, place them into a rigid mechanical/geometric transformation grammar, and make their interaction produce an output that is forced as much as possible by the permitted transformations.

So:

[
A\to R,\qquad B\to Q,
]

then

[
(R,Q)+\text{hard constraints}
\to C(R,Q).
]

The important idea is not “curves on a torus.” It is **constrained transformation with information preservation**.

And preservation need not mean keeping every constraint unchanged. A rule may be relaxed if the transformation itself carries the resulting structural difference intrinsically. The box-to-cylinder example belongs here.

Its potential outputs include genuine equivalence, weaker shared structure, common invariant, intersection regime, or explicit residual showing exactly what had to be changed.

The major unfinished job is reconstructing the exact correct mechanical implementation: rolling/contact constraints, forcing relation, output tracer, closure, reversibility, and uniqueness.

**4. Spheres**
Third independent solver.

This one should remain **provisional** until we do the same reconstruction. We know it involves interacting/overlapping spheres, evolving intersection loci, carrier circles, bifurcations, chained response, handedness, and transition rules. But we have not yet cleanly separated your intended machine from implementations and later physical assignments.

So I would not freeze its definition yet.

Then there is an important meta-category:

**5. Applications are not solvers.**

SAT/H(s)H particle geometry, pulsars, cosmology, paired space/time metrology, Lagrangians, quantum/classical correspondence, etc. are assignments made *using* these machines.

Likewise, (B=3/4\pi), (0.246), 24-cells, Z3, filament scales, particle identifications, and so on are not intrinsic properties of UI, Whirligig, or Spheres.

That boundary should be rigid.

And I think there’s a useful higher-level taxonomy:

[
\boxed{\text{UI: broad representational/control space}}
]

[
\boxed{\text{Whirligig: constrained comparison/transform engine}}
]

[
\boxed{\text{Spheres: constrained interaction/bifurcation engine?}}
]

The question mark on the last one is intentional.

That gives us three genuinely different tools rather than three visualizations of SAT.

And yes, I think these deserve development on their own terms. Even if SAT died tomorrow, a useful way to decompose arbitrary trajectories into scale/rotation histories, a mechanically constrained method for comparing mathematical representations, or a clean geometry-of-intersections solver could remain useful mathematics/computation.

My next move would be to create a **Solver Canon** containing only:

purpose, primitive objects, allowed operators, input types, output types, invariants, ambiguities/gauges, information-preservation rules, known failure modes, and validation tests.

No physics assignments at all.

And I would freeze UI first, Whirligig second, then reconstruct Spheres against the same template.
